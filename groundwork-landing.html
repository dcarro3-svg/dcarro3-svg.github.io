Stage 1 Relation Naming

Equal Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

Both bars are the same size. When two quantities match exactly, we call it an equal relation — the simplest and most fundamental relationship in mathematics.

1 of 5

Part-Whole Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

The top bar is the whole. The two bars below are the parts. Together, the parts equal the whole — they could be any size, as long as they add up.

2 of 5

Comparison Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

One bar is more, one is less. The gap between them is the difference. Comparisons always involve three quantities: the larger, the smaller, and the difference.

3 of 5

Fractional Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

The top bar is one whole. The equal boxes below show it split into parts. The filled box is one of those equal parts — one half, one third, one fourth.

4 of 5

Multiplicative Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

The whole holds 3 equal parts. So the whole is 3 times the size of one part — and one part is one third of the whole. The same picture tells both stories.

Stage 4 Curriculum Scope

Unit 1 — Simple Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

Five lessons covering equal, part-whole, comparison, fractional, and multiplicative relations. The vocabulary of mathematical structure — built from the ground up.

1 of 5 units

Unit 2 — Compound Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

Multi-step relations where two or more simple relations combine. Students learn to read nested structure and track multiple quantities at once.

2 of 5 units

Unit 3 — Complex Relations

[Explanation text placeholder]

Two-step chained relations. Students map connected quantities and identify the full relational structure before selecting a computation pathway.

3 of 5 units

Unit 4 — Word Problems

Applied practice with authentic problems. Students use relation naming as a reading comprehension strategy — identifying structure before computing.

The Premise

Students don't fail math
because it's too hard.

They fail because no one taught them to read it. A word problem describes a situation with a relational structure. Before a student can solve it, they need to comprehend it. Relation naming is that comprehension step.

Groundwork builds that foundation — lessons, fluency drills, and a progress chart that makes growth visible, for every student, every session.
